ROV electronics and over-the-air updates

E‑JUST Robotics Club, MATE ROV, 2022 to now

I designed the complete electronics of the club's competition ROV across five board generations: custom PCBs, power distribution, a three-tier control system and a CAN network that lets us flash new firmware without opening the hull. It won Best Communication System at MATE ROV Egypt 2025.

Everything topside and onboard shares one Ethernet network. The station PC connects over a single 1 Gb link to an Ethernet switch, and two cables leave that switch: one to the Raspberry Pi 5 and one to the STM32F411 gateway, which has its own W5500 Ethernet chip. The Pi runs ROS 2 Jazzy for pilot input, autonomy and logging, and talks plain ROS 2 with the station PC. The gateway is the only board running micro-ROS, and its agent runs on the station PC. Below the gateway, distributed boards on a 1 Mbps CAN bus drive the thrusters, measure current and run pressure compensation.

Project SPITFIRE, where it all started

Personal project, high school to now

Six foam airframes and three custom flight-controller PCBs, built from a Flite Test Spitfire because no flight controller was sold in Egypt. Almost everything else here grew out of it.

It began in high school with a Flite Test foam-board Spitfire Mk.VII. I needed a flight controller, but ArduPilot and Pixhawk weren't sold in Egypt at the time, so I designed my own, and rebuilt the airframe six times as I learned what each change did to lift, drag, control-surface sizing and balance.

Three PCB revisions carried it. V1 was the first board I ever made, an NRF24 radio receiver: traces too thin, a connector reversed, no decoupling caps, and it worked anyway. V2 was a proper revision with ground planes and bypass caps, running my own MultiWii-based receiver code. V3 went hybrid through-hole and SMD, compact enough to sit cleanly inside the fuselage. Around it I built retractable, servo-actuated landing gear, laser-cut internal formers, and a custom RC transmitter with hall-effect gimbals, so I understood every part of the link.

It placed second at the JAC-ECC Project-Based Learning competition, run by the IEEE E‑JUST Student Branch.

Industrial AGV with two lidars and a digital twin

Graduation project with MANEX Systems, 2026 to 2027

SLAM navigation from two lidars, a digital twin for development, and CAN bus between the main computer and the motor drivers. The next step after the line-follower I built at Nissan.

My graduation project at E‑JUST, built with the industry partner MANEX Systems. Where the Nissan AGV followed tape on the floor, this vehicle builds a map with two lidars and plans its own path.

The software stack runs SLAM on the main computer and keeps a digital twin of the vehicle for development and monitoring. Underneath, CAN bus is the low-level link between the main computer and the motor drivers, reusing the protocol work from the ROV.

Phase 1: the simulated warehouse

Phase 1 is a full simulation of the vehicle in a warehouse of racks and lanes. A mission-control interface holds the rack locations, a task queue (move this rack to that slot), manual teleop and lift up/down, with pause and emergency stop. The robot runs Nav2 on the same map you see in RViz, and the lifting mechanism is a scissor lift I designed and ran a motion study on in SolidWorks.

Nissan Egypt's First AGV

Nissan Motor Egypt, summer 2023

Researched, built and deployed an autonomous guided vehicle (AGV) in a two-month internship. It took over half a worker's material handling on every shift.

~4,000 EGPsaved per month, on every shift (about $130 at the 2023 rate)

27,000 EGPtotal build budget (about $870 at the 2023 rate)

Nissan Motor Egypt had never had an autonomous guided vehicle (AGV). During a two-month engineering internship across the MSPO, APW and Maintenance teams, I took the project from research to a vehicle running on the floor.

Before building, I tested the options side by side: IR line following against computer vision, electrical tape against magnetic tape, and DC, brushless and hoverboard hub motors compared on torque and cost. The deployed AGV took over half (50%+) of a worker's material handling on every shift, which saves about 4,000 EGP a month per shift (about $130), on a total build budget of 27,000 EGP (about $870). Autonomous navigation on ROS 2

RoboCup@Home Egypt, April 2026 · Mobile Robotics Lead

A Nav2 deployment with SLAM Toolbox, AMCL and fused lidar, odometry and IMU, tuned until mapping held up live. It became the club's reference setup.

This moved the club from underwater 3D control to autonomous driving on the ground. I built the Nav2 stack on a Pioneer 3-DX base with SLAM Toolbox for mapping, AMCL for localization, and sensor fusion of lidar, wheel odometry and IMU.

Most of the work was tuning: costmaps, the controller and planner servers, recovery behaviors and behavior trees, until live mapping and loop closure were dependable. A ZED stereo camera adds depth perception for object tasks, and custom udev rules keep the hardware mapping stable across machines.

To bring the robot into ROS 2 at all, I rewrote the legacy rosaria ROS 1 driver from scratch as ROS2Aria, now open source.

A paper form, rebuilt as a live dashboard

LEONI Egypt, Operational Excellence, August 2026

Line efficiency was tallied by hand every hour. I modelled 140,000+ barcode scans in Power BI so the form fills itself.

8 daysto learn SQL and Power BI and ship it

LEONI's packaging area tracked labour efficiency (KOSU, man-minutes per good part) on a paper form, with a supervisor tallying good and bad parts every hour. Every finished harness was already barcode-scanned before boxing, and that data was going unused.

I built a Power BI model on the scan data that reproduces the form live: the same layout, colour bands and maths, filterable by date, line and shift. I derived shift and hour from raw timestamps, wrote every DAX measure, and drove both the cell colours and the trend lines from one central thresholds table. The model was built against a read-only production database and connects to the live SQL source with no rework.

Separately, I ran a 6S audit of the packaging area and traced its repeat findings to one gap in the rack-labelling standard.

Conveyor sorting on a SIMATIC S7-1500

Siemens Industrial, TIA Portal training, 2026

Ladder logic with tracked part position, my own HMI screen, and a fault latch that only clears on a deliberate reset.

During Siemens' two-week SIMATIC Programming 1 course I designed a conveyor program on my own. Left, middle and right buttons send a part to the matching sensor. The part's position is stored as an explicit state in the ladder logic rather than guessed from sensors.

I built the HMI screen with a live view of the part, plant on and off, jog and error acknowledge. Pressing conflicting commands together latches the plant into a defined error state that shows on the HMI and only clears with a deliberate reset from the panel or the rig. I also ran a SINAMICS drive at set speeds and configured PROFINET devices.

A holonomic robot that an overhead camera drives

Mobile Robotics, E‑JUST, 2025

A four‑wheel omnidirectional base that gets its position not from wheel odometry, which drifts, but from a ceiling camera tracking a marker on its back.

A compact holonomic robot I built for the Mobile Robotics course. Four omni wheels let it translate in any direction and spin at the same time, with no need to turn before it moves.

Rather than dead‑reckoning from wheel encoders, the robot is localized by an off‑board camera looking down on the arena. The camera finds an ArUco marker on top of the robot and recovers its pose, which closes the loop: the controller compares that pose to the target and solves the holonomic kinematics for the four wheel speeds, so position error is corrected against ground truth instead of accumulating.

A homemade autopilot and ground station

Personal project · grew out of Project SPITFIRE

ArduPilot compiled from source onto a Raspberry Pi Zero with a carrier board I designed, flying waypoint missions with a Nextion ground station over HC-12.

When the SPITFIRE airframe needed real autonomy, I built the autopilot for it. ArduPilot's ArduPlane was compiled from source to run on a Raspberry Pi Zero through OBAL, an open Linux autopilot layer, on a carrier board I designed to break out the sensors and radios.

It flies pre-planned waypoint missions, streams live telemetry back over an HC-12 long-range radio link, and is commanded from a ground station I built into a hard case: a Nextion touchscreen HMI with my own aircraft graphics, a DIY multiprotocol transmitter and the mission uplink. I wrote the ground-station interface and the PPM/SBUS signal path that ties the radio to the flight controller.

Project STEALTH, a Reaper from foam and plywood

Personal project, 2022 to 2023

A scale MQ-9 Reaper drawn up in CorelDRAW and cut from foam and plywood, built to carry the autopilot from SPITFIRE. It never flew, but the fabrication and electronics were the point.

STEALTH is the fixed-wing build that followed SPITFIRE. I took published MQ-9 Reaper plans and turned them into my own set of cut sheets in CorelDRAW: fuselage sections in 3 and 5 mm polyfoam (Depron), 3 mm plywood formers, wing formers, wingtips, a motor mount and a landing-gear base, all nested for cutting.

The build covered three things: the foam and wood fabrication, a landing gear I developed for it, and the electronics, with my own radio transmitter and receiver firmware, and the ArduPilot autopilot from SPITFIRE carried over to it.

It never left the ground. I couldn't source brushless motors powerful enough for it in Egypt, so I treat it as a fabrication and electronics project rather than a flight result. and are the work it built on.
